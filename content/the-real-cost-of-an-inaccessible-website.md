A website that isn't accessible doesn't just look bad. For a meaningful share of visitors, it doesn't work at all. Screen readers, keyboard-only navigation and other assistive technology depend on a site being built with real structure underneath it, and when that structure is missing, the content might as well not exist for that visitor.

Semantic HTML is where this starts. Elements like header, nav, main and footer aren't just tidy code organization. They're how a screen reader understands what part of the page it's looking at. A div styled to look like a button isn't a button to assistive technology; an actual button element is. The same goes for headings: H1 through H6 aren't a font-size shortcut, they're a table of contents a screen reader user relies on to skip to the section they need instead of listening to an entire page read top to bottom.

Alt text, keyboard navigation and color contrast round out the basics, and all three get skipped constantly because a sighted user testing with a mouse never notices they're missing. Try tabbing through a site with the mouse unplugged sometime. If you can't reach every link and button, the focus outline disappears, or an image conveys information a screen reader has no way to announce, that's a real barrier for a real visitor, not an edge case.

This has become a legal question as much as a design one. The ADA in the US and the European Accessibility Act both treat an inaccessible website as a compliance issue, and lawsuits over exactly this have become common enough that "we didn't think about it" isn't much of a defense anymore. For sites handling healthcare or prescription information specifically, the stakes are higher still. Someone who can't reach that information because of a structural oversight isn't a minor UX complaint. It's a person who needed something and couldn't get to it.

None of the fixes here are exotic. Proper heading structure, real alt text, visible focus states, sufficient contrast, and a site that works with a keyboard alone cover most of what actually matters, and nearly all of it is cheaper to build in from the start than to retrofit later.
