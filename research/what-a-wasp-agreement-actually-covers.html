What a WASP agreement actually covers

A response plan written before a breach happens works. One written during it usually doesn't.

BS
Brent ScioneApr 3, 2026

Most small businesses have never heard of a WASP, and most of them are one data incident away from wishing they had one. A Web Application Security Plan is exactly what it sounds like: a written plan for how a business protects the data it collects through its website and responds if something goes wrong, decided calmly in advance instead of improvised during an actual breach.

The plan has a few real components, and none of them are exotic. A risk assessment that actually looks at where a site is vulnerable, not a generic checklist. Written security policies covering data protection, access control and who's allowed to touch what. An incident response plan that spells out the specific steps to take the moment a breach is suspected, so the first hour after discovery gets spent executing a plan instead of arguing about who's in charge of the response. And ongoing monitoring and audits, because a security posture that was fine a year ago isn't guaranteed to still be fine now.

The part that gets businesses in real trouble is the absence of that incident response plan specifically. When a breach happens without one, the first few hours get spent figuring out who needs to be notified, what the legal obligations even are, and who has the authority to make decisions, all while the actual problem is still ongoing. Regulations like GDPR and CCPA carry real notification timelines, and scrambling to learn what those timelines are during an active incident is how a bad situation turns into a legal one.

None of this requires a huge security budget to get right. A written plan, a designated point person, and a habit of actually reviewing both on a schedule covers most of what regulators and customers expect. The businesses that skip this entirely aren't saving money. They're deferring the cost to a moment when it'll be far more expensive to pay.
